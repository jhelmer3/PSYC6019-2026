What is Bayesian Inference

NHST is a decision rule framework for how we make a categorical choice: do we decide to reject the null hypothesis?

  • Bayesian inference is also a kind of decision rule, but it is not discrete, it is continuous.

  • Way of defining how we can use new data to updating our existing beliefs about what a population parameter is likely to be.

The Elements of Bayes’ Rule

Let’s imagine we have a coin, we want to determine the true proportion of times that the coin will land on heads (vs. tails).

  • We will call the coin’s true proportion of landing on heads (which is unknown) \(\pi\).
  • We then flip the coin 10 times, and observe 7 heads and 3 tails, which is our “data,” which we will call \(\boldsymbol{D}\).

The Elements of Bayes’ Rule

\[P(\theta|D) = \frac{P(D|\theta)P(\theta)}{P(D)}\]

  • \(P(\pi)\) - our prior belief about the proportion of times the coin will lands heads
    • Notice: The true \(\pi\) is unknown. This is a statement about our confidence it might take on certain values. So we might think it is very likely 50%, but there is some chance it is not, and is an unfair coin
  • \(P(\boldsymbol{D}|\pi)\) - The likelihood of observing 7 heads and 3 tails, given the (unknown) true \(\pi\).
  • \(P(\boldsymbol{D})\) - The probability of observing 7 heads and 3 tails unconditionally
    • One way to define this: \(P(\boldsymbol{D}) = \sum P(\boldsymbol{D}|\theta)P(\theta)\)
    • Really we typically need calculus for this: \(P(\boldsymbol{D}) = \int P(\boldsymbol{D}|\theta)P(\theta)\,d\theta\)
  • \(P(\theta | \boldsymbol{D})\) - the posterior belief about the proportion of times the coin will land on heads, incorporating both the prior and the data.

Probabilities of Probabilities

Notice that our prior is a probability statement about not about how likely the coin is to land on heads, but our confidence in the true proportion of times the coin will land on heads \(\pi\). If we are very confident, our prior might look something like this.

This plot suggests we think it’s very likely the coin is fair, though there’s some possibiliy is is weighted more heavily towards heads or tails.

Frequentists HATE him

This is the part frequentists object to. For a frequentist:

  • Only “random variables” can be represented by a probability distribution
  • The true proportion of heads is not a random variable
  • It is a single number, a cold hard fact, just one that we don’t know

For a Bayesian

  • Probability refers to what is known, not just what is true or not true

Bayseians treat probability as a psychological phenomenon

Frequentists treat probability as a metaphysical phenomenon

Two Takes on Probability

  • Frequentists: Parameters are facts about the world that are constant and can only actually be single values. The only things that are random are the data. We can’t reverse the probability arrow population \(\rightarrow\) sample
  • Bayesians: The only constants we can actually know are the facts we observe (data). Based on our limited knowledge, we need to a way to quantify how likely different possible parameters are. Probability is a general use language we use to describe the likelihood of different unknowns (parameters) based on our knowledge (data)

Which philosophical position do you take?

Posterior Belief

If we have a strong prior belief, seeing 7 heads and 3 tails should make us think if our coin happens to be unfair, it’s more likely to be weighted towards heads than tails. But seven heads and three tails aren’t that surprising for a fair coin, so our belief won’t change that much.

A Different Prior

What if an alien came to earth with no knowledge about how coins worked? They might have no idea the true proportion of times most coins land on heads, and think all possible values of \(\pi\) are equally likely

Alien’s Posterior Belief

In this case, the data might be quite persuasive! After seeing 7 heads and 3 tails, the alien might be fairly convinced that the coin was weighted towards heads.

How to Specify your Prior

In practice, priors are specified as probability distributions.

For example, if I am very confident the coin is likely to be fair, my prior might be a normal distribution with \(\mu = 0.5\) and \(\sigma = 0.1\).

  • \(\mu = 0.5\) means I think the true proportion of heads is most likely to be \(0.5\),
  • \(\sigma = 0.1\) means that in my mind, I am around 68% certain that the true proportion is between \(0.4\) and \(0.6\)
    • Because 68% of the values in a normal distribution are within 1 SD of the mean
  • Likewise, I am around 98% certain the true proportion is between \(0.3\) and \(0.7\)
    • Because 98% of the values in a normal distribution are within 2 SD of the mean

How to Specify your Prior

This prior would technically be improper. Can anyone see why?

A normal distribution can have values from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\), but a proportion can only be between 0 and 1

This is one of the reasons Bayesian Statsitics is more complex to learn.

You can use improper priors, but in general, proper priors are going to allow us to do more and more interesting things.

How to Incorportate Data

How does data move us from prior to posterior?

Through the likelihood function

\[P(\pi|\boldsymbol{D}) = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\pi)P(\pi)}{P(\boldsymbol{D})}\]

  • Prior: \(P(\pi)\)
  • Likelihood: \(P(\boldsymbol{D}|\pi)\)
  • Scaling term: \(P(\boldsymbol{D})\)
  • Posterior: \(P(\pi|\boldsymbol{D})\)

Likelihood Function of our Data

Maximum Likelihood

Recall: To find the maximum likelihood, we find the highest point on the hill

MAP Estimation

One approach to Bayesian estimation is very similar to Maximum Likelihood. It is known as Maximum A Posteriori estimation (MAP). Under MAP, we aren’t necessarily interested in the actual probability distribution \(P(\pi |\boldsymbol{D})\), just the value of \(\pi\) that maximizes this function.

Notice what this allows us to do with Bayes’ rule:

\[P(\theta|\boldsymbol{D}) = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\theta)P(\theta)}{P(\boldsymbol{D})} = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\theta)P(\theta)}{\int P(\boldsymbol{D}|\theta)P(\theta)\,d\theta}\]

Because we don’t care about the equality, only the maximum point, we can drop out the denominator, and instead say

\[P(\theta|\boldsymbol{D}) \propto P(\boldsymbol{D}|\theta)P(\theta)\]

Where \(\propto\) means “is proportional to”.

This is a common approach when the primary goal is making predictions, rather than making a confidence statement about parameter values.

General Form

The coin flip was a motivating example, but not very practice. Our general version of Bayes’ rule is:

\[P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{D}) = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{P(\boldsymbol{D})}\]

  • Prior: \(P(\boldsymbol{\theta})\)
  • Likelihood: \(P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\)
  • Scaling term: \(P(\boldsymbol{D})\)
  • Posterior: \(P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{D})\)

This allows us to consider our confidence in multiple parameters simultaneously, represented generically as \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\)

General Linear Model

For example, recall that we can rewrite a \(t\)-test in the form of a simple linear model

\[y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Group}_i + \epsilon_i\]

Our parameter space here is \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = [\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma_\epsilon]\)

  • \(\beta_0 =\) sample mean when \(\text{Group}_i = 0\)
  • \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 =\) sample mean when \(\text{Group}_i = 1\)
  • \(\sigma_\epsilon =\) shared standard deviation for each group (residual SD)

We want to estimate Bayes’ rule without losing the denominator, complex integral and all

\[P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\boldsymbol{D}) = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{P(\boldsymbol{D})} = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sum P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta})} = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int P(\boldsymbol{D}|\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}}\]

Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a simulation-based procedure for determining the goint of posterior distribution of all parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) when it’s too complicated to figure out numerically.

There are many different MCMC based algorithms. In a very general/intuitive sense, the way MCMC works is as follows:

  • We draw random values from \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) based on our prior, and check how well those values “fit” our likelihood
  • If the fit is not good enough we (might) throw away that draw (probabilistically) and make another one
  • If it is good enough, we keep it, and update our parameter estimates based on this value
  • We repeat this process many times, until we have a large “synthetic sample” of values that represent the combination of our prior and the likelihood
  • We use the simulated values that we kept as a representation of the posterior distribution \(P(\boldsymbol{\theta} | \boldsymbol{D})\)

MCMC: More Detail I

In our example, where \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = [\beta_0, \beta_1, \sigma_\epsilon]\)

Step 1: Choose starting values for each parameter as draws from their prior distributions. Call them \(\beta_{0_1}\), \(\beta_{1_1}\) and \(\sigma_{\epsilon_1}\).

Step 2: Draw a new random value for \(\beta_{0}\) from its prior distribution. Call this \(\beta_{0_2}\).

Step 3: Determine whether \(\beta_{0_1}\) or \(\beta_{0_2}\) is at a higher point on the likelihood function (data given parameters), with \(\beta_{1}\) assumed to be \(\beta_{1_1}\) and \(\sigma_{\epsilon}\) assumed to be \(\sigma_{\epsilon_1}\).

  • That is: Check whether \(\mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_2}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D}) \geq \mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_1}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D})\)

MCMC: More Detail II

Step 4: If

  • \(\mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_2}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D}) \geq \mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_1}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D})\), keep the proposed value \(\beta_{0_2}\) for sure.

  • \(\mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_2}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D}) < \mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_1}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D})\) only keep \(\beta_{0_2}\) with probability based on the ratio \(\frac{\mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_2}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1}; \boldsymbol{D})}{\mathcal{L}(\beta_{0_1}, \beta_{1_1}, \sigma_{\epsilon_1} ; \boldsymbol{D})}\)

Step 5: if \(\beta_{0_2}\) was kept, save it as a viable element of the posterior distribution of \(\beta_{0}\). Otherwise, throw it away.

Step 6: Draw a new random value for \(\beta_{1}\) from its prior distribution. Call this \(\beta_{1_2}\).

Step 7: Repeat steps 3-5, but for \(\beta_{1_2}\) rather than \(\beta_{0_2}\), and using \(\beta_{0_2}\) if it was accepted as a proposal, and \(\beta_{0_1}\) if it was not.

Step 8: Repeat steps 3-5 for \(\sigma_{\epsilon}\) using \(\beta_{0_2}\) and \(\beta_{1_2}\) if they were accepted as as a proposals, otherwise \(\beta_{0_1}\) and/or \(\beta_{0_1}\)

Repeat this entire process a pre=specified number of times. Eventually, the random draws should “converge” on a stable distribution that represents the posterior.

##

Point Estimates

The posterior distributions of each parameter can directly provide us inferential information.

Point estimates about parameters can be made using measures of central tendency

  • Mean: the posterior mean for a parameter is the value that will minimize squared error
  • Median: the posterior median for a parameter is the value that will minimize absolute error
  • Mode: “single” most likely parameter value

Credible Intervals

The spread of the distributions quantify confidence in parameter estimates, or plausible values parameters might take

  • Posterior SDs are a measure of confidence
  • Credible intervals are everything we wish confidence intervals were
    • It is correct to say a we are 95% certain the population value falls within a 95% credible interval
    • This is based on the likelihood (data) and prior (existing knowledge and confidence).
      • Important: if our prior wasn’t true or was poorly chosen this result will not hold!
      • Prior should represent true report of one’s prior belief
    • Contrast this with our earlier definitions of frequentist confidence intervals!
      • In 95% of samples, true parameter will fall inside interval
      • Range of values we cannot reject if treated as null hypothesis

Types of Credible Intervals

Equal Tailed Intervals (ETI):

  • Simply based on the symmetric quantiles of the distribution
  • E.g., for a 95% credible interval, 2.5% is outside in each of the upper and lower tails

Highest Posterior Density Intervals (HPD):

  • Proportion of the distribution such that all values outside of this range have less support than values inside
  • Not necessarily symmetrical in the tails

The two types will be equal in symmetric distributions, but will not be in asymmetric distributions. HPD is a better representation of the range best supported by the posterior (recommended)

Bayes Factors

Bayes Factors are a way of comparing the support for different models in a Bayesian framework.

They are ratios of posterior likelihoods between competing models. If we have two possible models for the same data, \(M_1\) and \(M_2\).

\[BF = \frac{P(\boldsymbol{D}|M_1)}{P(\boldsymbol{D}|M_2)}\]

  • When \(BF = 1\) the two models are equally likely
  • When \(BF > 1\) then \(M_1\) is more likely than \(M_2\)
    • When \(BF = 3\), \(M_1\) is three times as likely to be the “true” model as \(M_2\)
  • When \(BF < 1\), \(M_2\) is more likely than \(M_1\)
    • When \(BF = \frac{1}{3}\), \(M_2\) is three times as likely to be the “true” model as \(M_1\)

Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages:

  • Intuitive way to compare two (or more) models to one another
  • Fairly straightforward interpretation

Disadvantages:

  • Assume that the models being compared are THE models
    • No model is the “true” model!
  • Can lead to similar pitfalls as frequentist analysis
    • \(BF\) of 3 (or \(\frac{1}{3}\)) have been proposed as “cutoff” values. This is just as arbitrary as \(\alpha = .05\), and even sillier since Bayes Factors have a much more intuitive interpretation than \(p\)-values

Cross Validation

Cross validation (CV) methods slice data up into sections.

They use one part of data as a “training” sample, and another as a “testing” sample.

The downside to this is the loss of precision/power.

Leave one out (LOO) cross-validation takes all possible combinations of \(N-1\) observations, leaving one row out a time, and cross validates on that single observation.

Pareto Smoothed Importance Sampling (PSIS) provides a method for estimating LOO-CV in a tractable way, and can be quite useful (though not always feasible).

This is a great method when available though, since models are validated on data that wasn’t in the sample on which they were fit, which is a huge bonus for generalizability and avoiding overfit.

Priors

By default, rstanarm uses the following priors

  • For \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) the prior is based on a normal distribution with \(\sigma = 2.5\)
    • \(\sigma = 2.5\) is based on a transformed scale, using information about the variables in the model to convert to \(z\)-score units
    • The intercept \(\beta_0\) has \(\mu\) based on the overall mean
    • The slope \(\beta_1\) has \(\mu = 0\)
  • \(\sigma\) is given a prior based on an exponential distribution
