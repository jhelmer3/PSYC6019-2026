A two-tailed Welch-Satterthwaite \(t\)-test with \(\alpha = .05\) indicated a significant difference between conditions, \(t(51.9) = -2.29, p = .026\). Participants in the self-handicap condition correctly answered a lower percentage of items (\(N = 32, M = .47, SD = .17\)) than participants in the control condition (\(N = 29, M = .58, SD = .22\)), 95% CI of the difference \(= [0.01, 0.22]\).

The observed difference between groups was \(.58 - .47 = .11\). This is an effect size

  • Effect size: a statistical estimate of a magnitude of an effect rather than a test

Problem: How should we interpret this effect size?

Standardized Effect Size

In a vacuum, raw effect sizes are difficult to interpret and compare to one another

  • Often because the scale of the original variable(s) can be hard to directly interpret
  • What difference in proportion would be considered large?
  • Standardized effect sizes put all effect sizes on a common scale to aid interpretation and comparison

Two Families

Not that type of family!

Two Families of Effect

The \(D\)-family

  • Refers to standardized differences (or ratios)
  • Cohen’s \(d\)
  • Risk, Odds, and Risk and Odds Ratios

The \(R\)-family

  • Refers to standardized associations
  • Correlation (standardized covariance)
  • \(R^2\), \(\eta^2\), \(\omega^2\)
  • \(W\) and Cramer’s \(V\)

Cohen’s \(d\)

General form:

\[d = \frac{\text{raw difference}}{\text{standard deviation}}\]

Interpretation: Transforms raw difference into \(Z\) (standard deviation) units

One Sample

\[d = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s_x}\]

Difference between observed and null hypothesized mean divided by standard deviation

Paired Sample

\[d = \frac{\bar{D}}{s_D}\]

Difference in paired sample means divided by standard deviation

Independent Samples

\[d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p}\]

Difference in sample means divided by pooled standard deviation

  • Can use other SDs besides pooled depending on desired interpretation

Subtle Clarification

\(t\)-test statistics and Cohen’s \(d\) have similar forms. They often have the same numerator, but slightly different denominators

  • \(t\)-statistics divide by the standard error

One Sample

\[t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s_\bar{x}}\]

Paired Sample

\[d = \frac{\bar{D}}{s_\bar{D}}\]

Independent Samples

\[d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}}\]

  • \(d\)-effect sizes divide by standard deviation

One Sample

\[d = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s_x}\]

Paired Sample

\[d = \frac{\bar{D}}{s_D}\]

Independent Samples

\[d = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{s_p}\]

Benchmarks

Jacob Cohen (another very important/famous statistician) proposed the following (very loose!) benchmarks for \(d\):

  • \(d \approx .2\) is a small effect
  • \(d \approx .5\) is a medium effect
  • \(d \approx .8\) is a large effect

These are rough point estimates

  • \(d = .1\) would be very small
  • \(d = .3\) is small-to-medium

Just use this as a way to orient yourself, not as an objective rule

Risk and Odds

Risk and Odds are ratio-based \(d\)-family effect sizes for categorical variables

Below are some results from the Moderna Phase 3 clinical trial of the mRNA-1273 COVID vaccine (November, 2020)

Baden, L. R., El Sahly, H. M., Essink, B., Kotloff, K., Frey, S., Novak, R., … & Zaks, T. (2021). Efficacy and safety of the mRNA-1273 SARS-CoV-2 vaccine. New England Journal of Medicine, 384(5), 403-416.

COVID-19 No COVID-19 Total
Vaccine 11 14,123 14,134
Placebo 185 13,888 14,073
Total 196 28,011 28,207

Risk is the conditional probability of one outcome for a group

  • Risk(COVID-19 | Vaccine) \(= \frac{11}{14,134} = .0008 = .08\%\)

  • Risk(COVID-19 | Placebo) \(= \frac{185}{14,073} = .0131 = 1.31\%\)

Odds is the ratio of one outcome to another outcome for a group

  • Odds(COVID-19 | Vaccine) \(= \frac{11}{14,123} = .0008\)

  • Odds(COVID-19 | Placebo) \(= \frac{185}{13,888} = .0133\)

More Risk and Odds

COVID-19 No COVID-19 Total
Vaccine 11 14,123 14,134
Placebo 185 13,888 14,073
Total 196 28,011 28,207

Risk uses total # in vaccine group as the denominator

  • Risk(COVID-19 | Vaccine) \(= \frac{11}{14,134} = .0008 = .08\%\)

  • Conditional probability

Odds uses # of vaccinated individuals who did not contract COVID-19 as the denominator

  • Odds(COVID-19 | Vaccine) \(= \frac{11}{14,123} = .0008\)

  • If outcomes are equally probable, odds = 1

Risk and odds are not always similar - here they are because getting COVID-19 (in the given time frame) is highly unlikely in either group.

Risk Ratio

COVID-19 No COVID-19 Total
Vaccine 11 14,123 14,134
Placebo 185 13,888 14,073
Total 196 28,011 28,207

Risk ratio (also known as relative risk)

\[\text{Risk ratio} = \frac{\text{Risk (COVID-19 | Placebo)}}{\text{Risk (COVID-19 | Vaccine)}}\] \[= \left(\frac{185}{14,073}\right) \bigg/ \left(\frac{11}{14,134}\right) = 16.89\]

  • The risk of contracting COVID-19 without a vaccine is 16.89\(\times\) higher than if you get the Moderna vaccine

  • If the two risks are equal, then the risk ratio equals 1

Odds Ratio

Odds ratio

\[\text{Odds ratio} = \frac{\text{Odds (COVID-19 | Placebo)}}{\text{Odds (COVID-19 | Vaccine)}}\] \[= \left(\frac{185}{13,388}\right) \bigg/ \left(\frac{11}{14,123}\right) = 17.10\]

The odds of contracting COVID-19 without a vaccine is over 17\(\times\) higher than if you get the Moderna vaccine

Risk vs. Odds

In our example, risk (ratio) and odds (ratio) gave us very similar answers.

Mathematically, the difference between risk and odds is what quantity goes in the denominator

Risk of COVID-19 among vaccinated participants:

  • .08% of those vaccinated contracted COVID-19

  • 1.31% of those unvaccinated contracted COVID-19

  • Risks are probabilities: range from 0 to 1

Odds vs. Risk

Odds of contracting COVID-19 in vaccine condition:

  • Among those vaccinated, the ratio of those who contracted COVID-19 to those who did not attack is .008

  • Odds \(\approx\) 1 indicate roughly equally likely outcomes

  • Odds > 1 indicates higher probability for the group in the numerator

  • Odds < 1 indicates higher probability for the group in the denominator

  • The odds of no COVID-19 for those vaccinated is \(\frac{14,123}{11} = 1284\)

  • The odds of no COVID-19 for those unvaccinated is \(\frac{13,888}{185} = 75\)

    • A vaccinated person is 1284\(\times\) more likely to not contract COVID-19 than to do so, whereas an unvaccinated person is only 75\(\times\) more likely to not (in study’s given time frame, pre-variants)
