Lecture 25: Review of Matrix Algebra

Mark Himmelstein

What is a Matrix?

A matrix is an array with \(R\) rows and \(C\) columns. A matrix is typically denoted with an upper case bolded letter. For example

\[\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 & 6 \\ 12 & -4 & 2 \\ 7 & -15 & 72 \\ -4 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

Is a \(4 \times 3\) matrix.

Vectors and Scalars

A vector is an \(R \times 1\) matrix. You might think of a vector as being like a single column. A vector is typically denoted with a lower case bolded letter. For example

\[\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 12 \\ 7 \\ -4 \end{bmatrix}\]

A single number is referred to as a scalar. It is typically denoted with an unbolded lower case letter. For example

\[x = 5\]

Matrix Addition

Matrices of the same size can be added element-wise

Example:

\[\begin{bmatrix}1 & 4\\ 2 & 5\\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}7 & 10\\ 8 & 11\\ 9 & 12 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1+7 & 4+10\\ 2+8 & 5+11\\ 3+9 & 6+12 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}8 & 14\\ 10 & 16\\ 12 & 18 \end{bmatrix}\]

  • Results in a matrix of the same size

  • Addition is not defined if matrices are not the same size

Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication is more complicated than matrix addition.

  • DO NOT multiply element-wise

  • Order matters (commutative property does not hold) (\(\mathbf{AB} \neq \mathbf{BA}\))

  • The number of columns in the first matrix must equal the number of rows in the second matrix

  • Results in a matrix with the same number of rows as the first matrix and the same number of columns as the second matrix

If \(\mathbf{A}_{2\times 3}\) and \(\mathbf{B}_{3 \times 5}\):

  • \(\mathbf{AB}\) has 2 rows, 5 columns

  • \(\mathbf{BA}\) is not defined

If \(\mathbf{A}_{2\times 3}\) and \(\mathbf{B}_{3 \times 2}\):

  • \(\mathbf{AB}\) has 2 rows, 2 columns

  • \(\mathbf{BA}\) has 3 rows, 3 columns

Matrix Multiplication

Example: \(\mathbf{A}_{2\times 3}\), \(\mathbf{B}_{3\times 2}\)

\[\mathbf{AB} = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 5\\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}7 & 10\\8 & 11\\9 & 12\end{bmatrix} = \\ \begin{bmatrix}1\times7+3\times8+5\times9 & 1\times10+3\times11+5\times12\\ 2\times7+4\times8+6\times9 & 2\times10+4\times11+6\times12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}76 & 103\\ 100 & 136 \end{bmatrix}\]

Matrix Multiplication

Example: \(\mathbf{A}_{2\times 3}\), \(\mathbf{B}_{3\times 2}\)

\[\mathbf{BA} = \begin{bmatrix}7 & 10\\8 & 11\\9 & 12\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 3 & 5\\2 & 4 & 6\end{bmatrix} = \\ \begin{bmatrix}7\times1+10\times2 & 7\times3+10\times4 & 7\times5+10\times6\\ 8\times1+11\times2 & 8\times3+11\times4 & 8\times5+11\times6\\ 9\times1+12\times2 & 9\times3+12\times4 & 9\times5+12\times6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}27 & 61 & 95\\ 30 & 68 & 106\\ 33 & 75 & 117 \end{bmatrix}\]

Matrix Transpose

Matrix transpose:

  • Rows become columns, columns become rows

  • Denoted with the “\(T\)” superscript: \(\mathbf{A}^T\) is the transpose of \(\mathbf{A}\)

\[\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 4\\ 2 & 5\\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{A}^T=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2 & 3\\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\]

Multiplying a matrix by its transpose is a generalization of squaring

A symmetric matrix is a square matrix for which \(\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}\). Example:

\[\begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 & 2 & 3\\ 1 & 4 & 5 & 6\\ 2 & 5 & 7 & 8\\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\]

Matrix Inverse

\(\mathbf{I}\) is an identity matrix (generalization of the number ‘1’), a square matrix with 1’s on the main diagonal and 0’s on the off-diagonals. Example:

\[\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

Matrix inverse:

  • Defined only for square matrices

  • \(\mathbf{A}^{-1}\) is the matrix inverse of \(\mathbf{A}\)

  • \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}\)

Inverse Example

\[\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5 & -2\\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

Practice: Matrix Addition

Compute \(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}\).

\[\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 4 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 & -2 \\ 7 & -3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2+1 & -1+6 & 4+(-2) \\ 0+7 & 3+(-3) & 5+0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 & 2 \\ 7 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\]

Answer in R

We can create matrices in R with the matrix() function.

  • Wrap it around a vector with c()
  • Specify how many rows/columns to use
A <- matrix(c(2, -1, 4,
              0,  3, 5), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
B <- matrix(c(1,  6, -2,
              7, -3,  0), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
A + B
     [,1] [,2] [,3]
[1,]    3    5    2
[2,]    7    0    5

Practice: Multiplication

Let \(\mathbf{A}\) be \(2 \times 3\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) be \(3 \times 2\). Compute \(\mathbf{AB}\) and \(\mathbf{BA}\).

\[\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\]

Practice: Matrix Multiplication (Answer)

\[\mathbf{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} 2\times1+1\times0+0\times5 & 2\times2+1\times(-1)+0\times3 \\ -1\times1+3\times0+4\times5 & -1\times2+3\times(-1)+4\times3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 19 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\mathbf{BA} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\times2+2\times(-1) & 1\times1+2\times3 & 1\times0+2\times4 \\ 0\times2+(-1)\times(-1) & 0\times1+(-1)\times3 & 0\times0+(-1)\times4 \\ 5\times2+3\times(-1) & 5\times1+3\times3 & 5\times0+3\times4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 7 & 8 \\ 1 & -3 & -4 \\ 7 & 14 & 12 \end{bmatrix}\]

In R

In R matrix multiplication has a special operator: %*%

A <- matrix(c( 2, 1, 0,
              -1, 3, 4), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
B <- matrix(c(1,  2,
              0, -1,
              5,  3), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)
A %*% B   # 2 x 2
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    2    3
[2,]   19    7
B %*% A   # 3 x 3
     [,1] [,2] [,3]
[1,]    0    7    8
[2,]    1   -3   -4
[3,]    7   14   12

Practice: Transposition

Find \(\mathbf{X}^T\).

\[\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 4 & 7 \\ 5 & 1 & -6 \\ 2 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\]

\(\mathbf{X}\) is \(4 \times 3\), so \(\mathbf{X}^T\) is \(3 \times 4\):

\[\mathbf{X}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 5 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 & 1 & 8 \\ 2 & 7 & -6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}\]

Practice: Squaring

Compute \(\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\). What do you notice about the result?

\[\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+9+1 & 2+0-4 \\ 2+0-4 & 4+0+16 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 11 & -2 \\ -2 & 20 \end{bmatrix}\]

The result is a square, symmetric matrix: \((\mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A})^T = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}\). The diagonal entries are sums of squares.

Squaring in R

A <- matrix(c( 1, 2,
               3, 0,
              -1, 4), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)
t(A) %*% A
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]   11   -2
[2,]   -2   20
crossprod(A)   # equivalent shortcut for t(A) %*% A
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]   11   -2
[2,]   -2   20

Practice: Are These Matrices Inverses? (1)

Determine whether \(\mathbf{B}\) is the inverse of \(\mathbf{A}\).

\[\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\mathbf{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} 3\times2+1\times(-5) & 3\times(-1)+1\times3 \\ 5\times2+2\times(-5) & 5\times(-1)+2\times3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{I}\]

The product is the identity matrix, so yes, \(\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\).

Answer (1) in R

A <- matrix(c(3, 1,
              5, 2), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
B <- matrix(c( 2, -1,
              -5,  3), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
A %*% B
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    1    0
[2,]    0    1
B %*% A      # order doesn't matter for an inverse
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    1    0
[2,]    0    1
solve(A)     # R computes the inverse directly
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]    2   -1
[2,]   -5    3

Practice: Are These Matrices Inverses? (2)

Determine whether \(\mathbf{D}\) is the inverse of \(\mathbf{C}\).

\[\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{D} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\mathbf{CD} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\times4+2\times3 & 1\times2+2\times1 \\ 3\times4+4\times3 & 3\times2+4\times1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 4 \\ 24 & 10 \end{bmatrix} \neq \mathbf{I}\]

The product is not the identity matrix, so no, \(\mathbf{D}\) is not the inverse of \(\mathbf{C}\).

Answer (2) in R

C <- matrix(c(1, 2,
              3, 4), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
D <- matrix(c(4, 2,
              3, 1), nrow = 2, byrow = TRUE)
C %*% D
     [,1] [,2]
[1,]   10    4
[2,]   24   10
solve(C)     # the actual inverse of C
     [,1] [,2]
[1,] -2.0  1.0
[2,]  1.5 -0.5
