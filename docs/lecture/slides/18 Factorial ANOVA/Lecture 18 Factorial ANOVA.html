Lecture 18: Factorial ANOVA

Mark Himmelstein

Review

  • Independent Sample \(t\)-tests compares the means of two different groups
    • E.g. One control group vs one experimental treatment
  • One-Way ANOVA compares the means of more than two different groups from a single factor
    • E.g. One control group vs more than one different experimental treatments
  • Factorial ANOVA (Two-Way, Three-Way, etc.) compares the means of different groups based on combinations of different factors
    • E.g. Three different drug types at two different dosage levels.

Preview

In a Two-Way ANOVA, there are two types of effects:

  1. Interaction Effect
    • The effect of factor \(A\) on the outcome depends on factor \(B\)
    • The effect of factor \(B\) on the outcome depends on factor \(A\)
  2. Main Effects (also sometimes marginal effects)
    • The effect of factor \(A\) on the outcome, combining levels of factor \(B\)
    • The effect of factor \(B\) on the outcome, combining levels of factor \(A\)

Traditional Structure

For the two-way ANOVA, there are two categorical IVs (factors)

Factor \(A\) has levels \(j = 1, \ldots, J\) and Factor \(B\) has levels \(k = 1, \ldots, K\)

Population model:

\[y_{ijk} = \mu_G + \alpha_j + \beta_k + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} + \epsilon_{ijk}\]

  • \(y_{ijk}\): Observed value of observation \(i\) belonging to level \(j\) of factor \(A\) and level \(k\) of factor \(B\)
  • \(\mu_{G}\): Population grand mean (disregarding group membership)
  • \(\alpha_j = \mu_{A_j} - \mu_G\): effect of factor \(A_j\)
  • \(\beta_k = \mu_{B_k} - \mu_G\): effect of factor \(B_k\)
  • \((\alpha\beta)_{jk} = \mu_{AB_{jk}} - \mu_{A_j} - \mu_{B_k} + \mu_G\): interaction of factor \(A_j\) and factor \(B_k\)
  • \(\epsilon_{ijk}\): deviation of observation \(i\) from the mean of observations in both group \(A_j\) and group \(B_k\)

Parsing Variance

Population model (from the previous slide):

\[y_{ijk} = \mu_G + \alpha_j + \beta_k + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} + \epsilon_{ijk}\]

We can write:

\[\text{var}(y) = \text{var}(\alpha) + \text{var}({\beta}) + \text{var}(\alpha\beta) + \text{var}(\epsilon)\]

  • Total score variance can be attributed to these 4 sources
  • Important: variance can only be parsed in this manner if all combinations of groups have equal numbers of observations
    • We will return to “unbalanced” cases later

ANOVA Calculations I

\(y_{ijk}\): observation \(i\) in group \(j\) of factor \(A\) and group \(k\) of factor \(B\)

  • \(\bar{y}_{jk}\): cell mean for combination of \(j\) (fct \(A\)) and \(k\) (fct \(B\))
  • \(\bar{y}_{j\bullet}\): marginal mean of group \(j\) (fct A), across levels of fct B

\[\bar{y}_{j\bullet} = \frac{\sum_k\sum_iy_{ijk}}{\sum_kn_{jk}} = \frac{\sum_k\bar{y}_{jk}}{\sum_kn_{jk}}\]

  • \(\bar{y}_{\bullet k}\): marginal mean of group \(k\) (fct B), across levels of fct A

\[\bar{y}_{\bullet k} = \frac{\sum_j\sum_iy_{ijk}}{\sum_jn_{jk}} = \frac{\sum_j\bar{y}_{jk}}{\sum_jn_{jk}}\]

ANOVA Calculations II

  • \(\bar{y}\): overall mean across participants and groups (sample grand mean)

\[\bar{y} = \frac{\sum_k\sum_j\sum_iy_{ijk}}{N}\]

ANOVA Calculations III

For a \(2\times 3\) design:

Factor \(B\) Factor \(A\): \(j=1\) Factor \(A\): \(j=2\) Factor \(A\): \(j=3\) Marginal Mean for Factor \(B\)
\(k=1\) \(\bar{y}_{11}\) \(\bar{y}_{21}\) \(\bar{y}_{31}\) \(\bar{y}_{1\bullet}=\frac{\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j1}}{\sum_{j}n_{jk}}\)
\(k=2\) \(\bar{y}_{12}\) \(\bar{y}_{22}\) \(\bar{y}_{32}\) \(\bar{y}_{2\bullet}=\frac{\sum_{j}\bar{y}_{j2}}{\sum_{j}n_{jk}}\)
Marginal Mean for Factor \(A\) \(\bar{y}_{\bullet1}=\frac{\sum_{k}\bar{y}_{1k}}{\sum_{k}n_{jk}}\) \(\bar{y}_{\bullet2}=\frac{\sum_{k}\bar{y}_{2k}}{\sum_{k}n_{jk}}\) \(\bar{y}_{\bullet3}=\frac{\sum_{k}\bar{y}_{3k}}{\sum_{k}n_{jk}}\) Overall Mean: \(\bar{y}=\frac{\sum_{j}\sum_{k}\bar{y}_{jk}}{N}\)

More Calculations

We denote our estimated effects based on our sample statistics with a hat, e.g., \(\hat{\alpha}\) or \(\hat{\alpha\beta}\).

\[\hat{\alpha}_{east} = \bar{y}_{1\bullet}- \bar{y} =87.6-88.7= -1.1\]

\[\hat{\beta}_{control} = \bar{y}_{\bullet1}-\bar{y} =83.6-88.7= -5.1\]

\[\hat{\alpha\beta}_{east,control} = \bar{y}_{11} - \bar{y}_{1\bullet} - \bar{y}_{\bullet1} + \bar{y}=85.4 - 83.6 - 88.7 + 87.6 = \\0.7\]

Decomposition I

Similar to the one-way ANOVA, we can decompose the sample variation of \(y\) into within-group effects (\(W\)) and between-group (\(G\)) effects

\[SS_T = SS_G + SS_W\]

\(SS_T\): overall variation of scores

\[SS_T = \sum_k\sum_j\sum_i(y_{ijk} - \bar{y})^2\]

\(SS_G\): variation between groups

(between group effect)

\[SS_G = \sum_k\sum_j[n_{jk}(\bar{y}_{jk} - \bar{y})^2]\]

  • systematic variation
  • extent to which factors predict the DV scores

\(SS_W\): variation within groups

(within-group effect)

\[SS_W = SS_T - SS_G\]

  • residual (leftover) variation
  • extent to which DV scores cannot be predicted

Decomposition II

Sums of squares of the between-group effect (\(SS_G\)) can be decomposed into:

  • Main effect \(A\)
  • Main effect \(B\)
  • Interaction effect \(AB\)

\[SS_G = SS_A + SS_B + SS_{AB}\]

Decomposition III

\(SS_A\)

sum of squares for factor \(A\)

\[SS_A = nK\sum_j(\bar{y}_j - \bar{y})^2\]

= systematic variation in outcome associated with factor \(A\), across levels of factor \(B\)

\(SS_B\)

sum of squares for factor \(B\)

\[SS_B = nJ\sum_k(\bar{y}_k - \bar{y})^2\]

= systematic variation in outcome associated with factor \(B\), across levels of factor \(A\)

\(SS_{AB}\)

sum of squares for interaction effect

\[SS_{AB} = SS_G - SS_A - SS_B\]

These calculations assume that every group has \(n = n_{jk}\) subjects

  • \(J\): number of groups in factor \(A\)
  • \(K\): number of groups in factor \(B\)

More Calculations

Effect / Source Sums of Squares Degrees of Freedom Mean Squares
Between-Gp Effect \(SS_{G}=\sum_{k}\sum_{j}[n_{jk}(\bar{y}_{jk}-\bar{y})^{2}]\) \(df_{G}=(J\times K-1)\) \(MS_{G}=\frac{SS_{G}}{df_{G}}\)
Main Effect \(A\) \(SS_{A}=nK\sum_{j}(\bar{y}_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}\) \(df_{A}=J-1\) \(MS_{A}=\frac{SS_{A}}{df_{A}}\)
Main Effect \(B\) \(SS_{B}=nJ\sum_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}-\bar{y})^{2}\) \(df_{B}=K-1\) \(MS_{B}=\frac{SS_{B}}{df_{B}}\)
Interaction Effect \(SS_{AB}=SS_{G}-SS_{A}-SS_{B}\) \(df_{AB}=(J-1)(K-1)\) \(MS_{AB}=\frac{SS_{AB}}{df_{AB}}\)
Within-Gp Effect \(SS_{W}=SS_{T}-SS_{G}\) \(df_{W}=df_{T}-df_{G}\) \(MS_{W}=\frac{SS_{W}}{df_{W}}\)
Total Effect \(SS_{T}=\sum_{k}\sum_{j}\sum_{i}(y_{ijk}-\bar{y})^{2}\) \(df_{T}=N-1\) \(MS_{T}=\frac{SS_{T}}{df_{T}}\)

Hypothesis Testing

We begin by testing the interaction effect

Why?

Hypothesis Testing

Is the effect of our grape growing method the same, or different for different sides of the hill?

Interaction Testing

We begin by testing the interaction effect because an interaction means the effect of one IV depends on the other IV. Testing the main (overall) effect of that IV may not make sense if that effect varies based on another factor.

  • \(H_0\): there is no interaction effect in the population
  • \(H_1\): there is an interaction effect in the population

Test statistic: \(F\)-statistic

\[F_{df_{AB}, df_W} = \frac{MS_{AB}}{MS_W}\]

When \(H_0\) is true, \(F\) follows an \(F\)-distribution with \(df_{AB}\) numerator and \(df_W\) denominator degrees of freedom

  • If the interaction \(H_0\) is rejected, probe the interaction effect (later)
  • If the interaction \(H_0\) is not rejected, look at hypothesis tests for main effects

Main \(A\) Testing

Testing Main Effect of Factor \(A\):

  • \(H_0\): all \(\mu_j\) equal (no main effect of Factor \(A\))
  • \(H_1\): at least one \(\mu_j\) is different (main effect of Factor \(A\))

\[F(df_A, df_W) = \frac{MS_A}{MS_W}\]

When \(H_0\) is true, \(F\) follows an \(F\)-distribution with \(df_A\) numerator and \(df_W\) denominator degrees of freedom

Main \(B\) Testing

Testing Main Effect of Factor \(B\):

  • \(H_0\): all \(\mu_k\) equal (no main effect of Factor \(B\))
  • \(H_1\): at least one \(\mu_k\) is different (main effect of Factor \(B\))

\[F(df_B, df_W) = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W}\]

When \(H_0\) is true, \(F\) follows an \(F\)-distribution with \(df_B\) numerator and \(df_W\) denominator degrees of freedom

Main Interpretations

What does the main effect of factor \(A\) “mean”?

  • At the average value across all levels of factor \(B\) in our experiment/data/design, there are (or are not) differences in \(y\) for for the levels of factor \(A\)

When might this be weird or not make sense to do?

  • If all levels of factor \(B\) that we care about are not present in our data!
  • We can’t necessarily infer what would happen to our growing methods if north/south facing slopes were included. Our main effect estimate of growing method only considers the average of east and west

Interpreting Results

The Wine example had a significant interaction effect, \(F(2, 294) = 8.89, p < .001\)

  • We might continue investigating simple effects (later)

Given a significant interaction effect, it is not very meaningful to investigate the main effects of factor \(A\) or factor \(B\)

If the interaction effect is not significant, we can look at the hypothesis tests for main effects

  • There is a significant main effect of hill, \(F(1, 294) = 24.04, p < .001\)
  • There is a significant main effect of task type, \(F(2, 294) = 88.31, p < .001\)
  • Post-hoc tests can then be used to understand significant main effects

ANOVA Assumptions

Same assumptions as one-way ANOVA

  1. Normally distributed outcome in each group (in each combination of factor levels)
  2. No outliers in each group (in each combination of factor levels)
  3. Homogeneity of variance
    • Population variance is equal in every group (in every combination of factor levels)
  4. Independence of observations
    • All groups are independent of each other
    • If not independent, repeated measures ANOVA or multilevel modeling may be more appropriate (later in this course)

Same methods as one-way ANOVA can be used to investigate assumptions

ANOVA Assumptions

When homogeneity of variance is violated:

  • Welch’s adjustment is unavailable for two-way ANOVA
  • White’s adjustment is available
  • Weighted least squares (WLS) estimation is available
    • Each participant has weight \((1 / s^2_{jk})\)
    • \(s^2_{jk}\): sample variance for level \(j\) of factor \(A\) and level \(k\) of factor \(B\)

Recommendations

  • Use White and/or WLS for ALL the analyses
    • Hypothesis tests and effect sizes for interaction effect & main effects
    • Hypothesis tests and effect sizes for simple main effects
    • Post-hoc comparisons
      • Tukey HSD (recommended), Bonferroni, Holm are available
      • Games-Howell, Dunnett not available for factorial ANVOA
    • A priori comparisons
  • All calculations follow the standard procedures, except that we now use WLS estimation or White’s adjustment

APA Style Writeup

We conducted a two-way ANOVA to investigate the effects of hill side and growing method on wine ratings. At \(\alpha = .05\) and using White’s correction, the interaction effect was statistically significant (\(F(2, 294) = 9.17, p < .001\)).

To inspect the interaction effect, we calculated simple main effects…

Effect Sizes

\(r\)-family measures (standardized association):

  1. \(\eta^2\) and partial \(\eta^2\)
  2. \(\omega^2\) and partial \(\omega^2\)
  3. \(f\) (function of partial \(\eta^2\))

\(d\)-family measures:

  1. Cohen’s \(d\) for pairwise comparisons
  2. Appropriate for post-hoc tests of main effects or simple main effects

Partial \(\eta^2\)

Interpretation of partial \(\eta^2\):

  • Proportion of variation in outcome accounted for by the specified effect, ignoring other effects

Partial \(\eta^2\) for main effect of factor \(A\)

\[\frac{SS_A}{SS_A + SS_W}\]

Partial \(\eta^2\) for main effect of factor \(B\)

\[\frac{SS_B}{SS_B + SS_W}\]

Partial \(\eta^2\) for interaction effect

\[\frac{SS_{AB}}{SS_{AB} + SS_W}\]

\(SS_T = SS_G + SS_W\)

\(SS_T = (SS_A + SS_B + SS_{AB}) + SS_W\)

Note that the denominator of \(\eta^2\) only includes \(SS_W\) and specified effect

“Full” \(\eta^2\)

Interpretation of \(\eta^2\):

  • Proportion of total variation in the outcome accounted for by the specified effect

\(\eta^2\) for main effect of factor \(A\)

\[\frac{SS_A}{SS_T}\]

\(\eta^2\) for main effect of factor \(B\)

\[\frac{SS_B}{SS_T}\]

\(\eta^2\) for interaction effect

\[\frac{SS_{AB}}{SS_T}\]

Comparisons between partial \(\eta^2\) and \(\eta^2\)

  • We might prefer \(\eta^2\) because it uses the same denominator for the main effects and interaction. The three \(\eta^2\)s are directly comparable
  • We might prefer partial \(\eta^2\) because the denominator ignores other effects
  • \(\eta^2\) always smaller than partial \(\eta^2\)

\(\omega^2\)

Both \(\eta^2\) and partial \(\eta^2\) are positively biased estimates of the population effect size

\(\omega^2\) and partial \(\omega^2\) are less biased:

\(\omega^2\) for main effect \(A\)

\[\frac{df_A(MS_A - MS_W)}{SS_T + MS_W}\]

\(\omega^2\) for main effect \(B\)

\[\frac{df_B(MS_B - MS_W)}{SS_T + MS_W}\]

\(\omega^2\) for interaction

\[\frac{df_{AB}(MS_{AB} - MS_W)}{SS_T + MS_W}\]

Partial \(\omega^2\) for main effect \(A\)

\[\frac{df_A(MS_A - MS_W)}{SS_A + (N - df_A)MS_W}\]

Partial \(\omega^2\) for main effect \(B\)

\[\frac{df_B(MS_B - MS_W)}{SS_B + (N - df_B)MS_W}\]

Partial \(\omega^2\) for interaction

\[\frac{df_{AB}(MS_{AB} - MS_W)}{SS_T + (N - df_{AB})MS_W}\]

\(\omega^2\) (Omega-Squared) and Partial \(\omega^2\)

  • It is possible for partial \(\omega^2\) and \(\omega^2\) to be negative
  • This occurs when \(MS_{\text{effect}} < MS_W\) (numerator)
  • This affects interpretation. Suggestions:
    • Do not report the negative value
    • Correct the value to \(< .001\)
      • Like \(p\)-value cannot technically be 0

Effect Size: \(f\)

  • More traditionally used for power calculations

  • Function of partial \(\eta^2\) (not \(\eta^2\))

Definition:

\[f = \sqrt{\frac{\eta^2}{1 - \eta^2}}\]

Cohen’s (1988, 1992) suggestions:

  • 0.10: small effect (\(\eta^2 = 0.010\))
  • 0.25: medium effect (\(\eta^2 = 0.059\))
  • 0.40: large effect (\(\eta^2 = 0.138\))

General Linear Model Form

How might we write a factorial ANOVA as a general linear model?

  • What do we have to do to the factors first?
  • Give them a coding scheme

Defining Factors

Hill side has only two levels, so I will set them as

  • If hill = East, \(z = 0\)
  • If hill = West, \(z = 1\)

I will then code growing method as

Control A B
\(x_1\) 0 1 0
\(x_2\) 0 0 1

Allowing me to write a linear model as:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 z + \beta_2 x_1 + \beta_3 x_2 + \beta_4 z x_1 + \beta_5 z x_2 + \epsilon\]

Bayes Factor

As in one-way ANOVA, there closest direct corollary to omnibus tests of interactions and main effects in the Bayesian framework is to use Bayes factors.

library(rstanarm)
library(bayestestR)
options(mc.cores = parallel::detectCores())

Model 1

  • We might compare a model that includes the interactions:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 z + \beta_2 x_1 + \beta_3 x_2 + \beta_4 z x_1 + \beta_5 z x_2 + \epsilon\]

stan_lm1 <- stan_glm(rating ~ hill * method, dat, family = "gaussian",
                     diagnostic_file = "df1.csv", seed = 15421)

Model 2

  • To one that does not:

\[y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 z + \beta_2 x_1 + \beta_3 x_2 + \epsilon\]

stan_lm2 <- stan_glm(rating ~ hill + method, dat, family = "gaussian",
                     diagnostic_file = "df2.csv", seed = 32105)

Calculate Bayes Factor

set.seed(51021)
(bfs1 <- bayesfactor_models(stan_lm2, stan_lm1))

APA Writeup

We conducted a Bayesian two-way ANOVA to investigate the effects of hill side and growing method on wine ratings. Bayes Factors \(BF = 5.34\) in favor of the model that included the interaction between hill type and growing method over one that did not, indicating this model is 5.34 times as likely to be the true model as the non-interactive model.
