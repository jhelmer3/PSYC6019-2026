      • Test of a hypothesis about a continuous variable
      • Were our sampled NBA players consistent with a population that scored 12 points per game?
  • The \(Z\)-test allows us to test a specific hypothesis about a continuous variable, but assumes we know the population value of \(\sigma\)
    • Major limitation! But good for building a conceptual foundation

NHST Procedure

  1. Specify null hypothesis \(H_0\)
  2. Specify Sampling distribution of test statistic based on the null hypothesis
    • Test statistic: Some transformation of one or more descriptive statistics from your sample that correspond to a research question
    • E.g.: Transform sample mean to a \(Z\)-score based on the mean defined by the null hypothesis and the standard error
  3. Specify \(\alpha\) value
    • Value such that, if the test statistic is sufficiently improbable based on our sampling distribution, we reject the null hypothesis
    • Usually \(\alpha = .05\)
    • Arbitrary convention adopted because of off-the-cuff comment by Fisher How “how unlikely” is “sufficiently unlikely”

More NHST Procedure

  1. Determine probability of observing a test statistic as or more unlikely as the test statistic obtained from your sample under the null hypothesis
    • I.e., what proportion of test statistics would be as or more extreme
    • This is a \(p\)-value
    • Represents the probability of our test statistic (or any test statistic less likely than our test statistic) assuming the null hypothesis is true
    • If sampling distribution is symmetrical, might include chance of observing of equally unlikely outcome in opposite direction
      • Means we multiply \(p \times 2\)

Even More NHST Procedure

  1. If \(p > \alpha\), do not reject null hypothesis
    • Choose to believe Null hypothesis remains a plausible explanation for our observed statistic
  2. If \(p \leq \alpha\), reject null hypothesis
    • Choose to believe Null hypothesis is implausible explanation for our observed statistic

Dated Reframing

Before it was easy to compute exact \(p\)-values with computers, an alternative approach was more common

  • Define the critical value: value of test statistic that corresponds to \(\alpha = .05\)
    • If null is true, 5% of test statistics would be as or more extreme than this value
    • For a standard normal distribution with \(\alpha = .05\):
      • \(Z\) = 1.68 is the critical value for a one-tailed test
      • \(Z\) = 1.96 is the critical value for a two-tailed test
  • For lower tailed test: if test statistic \(<\) critical value, reject
  • For upper tailed test: if test statistic is \(>\) critical value, reject
  • For two-tailed test: if |test statistic| \(>\) critical value, reject
    • Observed mean that is 1.96 (or more) standard errors away from the null hypothesis is more extreme than critical value

Example

Research question: Do NBA players who play the shooting guard position score more than 12 points per game (PPG) on average

  • Maybe 12 PPG is based on some knowledge of historical averages I have
    • Maybe I also know historically the standard deviation of player PPG is \(\sigma = 6\) (usually won’t know stuff like this)
  • I want to determine if shooting guards beat historical averages
  • I sample 44 shooting guards
  • My sample scores 14.5 PPG
  • How unlikely would a random sample of players be to score 14.5 PPG if their true population had \(\mu = 12\), \(\sigma = 6\)?
    • \(H_0: \mu = 12\)
    • How many standard errors away from \(12\) is \(14.5\)?

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

  • Fisher’s framework focused on the null hypothesis
    • Did not include alternative hypothesis
    • Emphasized that \(\alpha\) cutoff value was arbitrary
    • Focused more on proper interpretation of \(p\)-value as probability of a statistic if the null is true
    • Small values as evidence against null
  • Alternative hypothesis introduced later by Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson
    • Built around deciding between competing hypotheses
    • \(H_0\) (null) specified as equality
      • \(H_0: \mu = 12\) or \(H_0: \mu \leq 12\) \(H_a\) (alternative) specified as inequality
      • \(H_a: \mu \neq 12\) or \(H_a: \mu > 12\)
    • Introduced Type I, II errors, power (more later)

Modern methods are weird hybrid between Fisher and Neyman/Pearson

\(\chi^2\) tests

Today we will discuss:

  • The \(\chi^2\) goodness of fit test allows us to test a hypothesis about the probability of each category for a categorical variable in the population
    • E.g. are each flavor of gummy bear equally common?
  • The \(\chi^2\) test of independence allows us to test if membership in one category is independent or membership in another category.
    • E.g. is the sentence for a crime independent of the defendant’s race?

Back to Gummy Bears

Two Hypotheses

In our gummy bear examples we’ve only thought about Pineapple so far. Now let’s consider each different flavor.

  • Equal Categories (EC): All flavors are distributed evenly (\(\frac{1}{5}\) chance each, equiprobable hypothesis)

But I secretly suspect that instead:

  • Too many raspberries (TMR): The candymakers are biased in favor of raspberry (\(\frac{1}{3}\) chance) over the other flavors (\(\frac{1}{6}\) chance each)

I buy a party sized back of 354 bears and randomly split it into two halves of 177 pieces each to independently test each hypothesis.

Sample 1

Under the EC hypothesis, in repeated samples, my expected frequency of each flavor would be:

\[f_{E1} = f_{E2} = f_{E3} = f_{E4} = f_{E5} = \frac{1}{5}177 = 35.4\]

However, my observed frequencies are:

\[f_{O1} =62,f_{O2} = 26, f_{O3} = 29, = f_{O4} = 28, f_{O5} = 32\]

How unlikely are are observed frequencies under the hypothesis that the expected frequencies are the true frequencies in the population?

Constructing our Sampling Distribution

We could look at how different our observed frequencies are from our expected frequencies, but if we just do that and sum them up, what would happen?

  • Instead we are going to look at squared differences

  • We are also going to normalize the squared differences by dividing by the expected frequencies

  • For each category, we will calculate \(k\)

\[\frac{(F_{Ok} - F_{Ek})^2}{F_{Ek}}\]

The \(\chi^2\) Distribution

Under the CLT, each of these will separately be distributed standard normal squared

  • Take every value in a normal distribution with \(\mu = 0\), \(\sigma = 1\) and square them

The sum of multiple squared random standard normal variables has a known distributional form of it’s own: the \(\chi^2\) distribution.

  • Any \(\chi^2\) distribution has degrees of freedom equal to the number of elements that can freely vary.
    • How many of our flavors can freely vary, assuming we hold the total number of gummy bears (\(N = 177\)) constant?

The Messy Truth

The \(\chi^2\) distribution has the following density function:

\[f_{df}(\chi^2) = \frac{(\frac{1}{2})^{df/2}}{\Gamma(\frac{df}{2})} \chi^{2(df/2 - 1)} e^{-\chi^2/2}\]

  • \(df\): degrees of freedom
  • \(\Gamma\): the gamma function

Fortunately, we never need to use this equation directly

The sum of \(df\) squared independent standard normal variables follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(df\) degrees of freedom

  • The \(\chi^2\) distribution is defined on the interval \([0, \infty]\)
  • \(\mathcal{E}(\chi^2) =\) mean \(\chi^2\) value = \(df\)
  • \(\text{Var}(\chi^2) = 2df\)

\(\chi^2\) Distributions

Sampling \(\chi^2\)s

If our EC hypothesis is true, that means that in repeated random samples, if we calculate

\[\chi^2 = \sum_{k=1}^K \frac{(F_{Ok} - F_{Ek})^2}{F_{Ek}}\]

The \(\chi^2\) statistic that results will follow a \(\chi^2\) distribution with \(df = K - 1\)

Under Fisher’s \(\alpha = .05\) convention, if there is less than a 5% chance that we would obtain a \(\chi^2\) statistic at least as large as the one we observe under this distribution, we reject the proposed hypothesis.

\(\chi^2\) Critical Value

The EC Hypothesis

In my first sample of gummy bears, I have 62 raspberries, 26 pineapples, 29 lemons, 28 oranges, and 32 strawberries

\[\chi^2_{\rm{obs}} = \frac{(62 - 35.4)^2}{35.4} + \frac{(26 - 35.4)^2}{35.4} + \frac{(29 - 35.4)^2}{35.4} + \frac{(28 - 35.4)^2}{35.4} + \frac{(32 - 35.4)^2}{35.4} = {25.51}\]

pchisq(25.51, df = 4, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 3.972231e-05

The probability of obtaining a \(\chi^2\) statistic of at least 25.51 from a \(\chi^2\) distribution with four degrees of freedom is \(3.97 \times 10^{-5}\) (VERY unlikely)

Thus, we reject the EC hypothesis.

Independence

In the \(\chi^2\) test of independence, we want to know if two categorical variables are related (dependent on each other)

\[H_0: \text{Categorical variables are independent}\]

\[H_1: \text{Categorical variables are related}\]

How do we define independence?

Notation

We next need to calculate expected frequencies based on \(H_0\)

First, a bit of notation: We often index rows with a subscript i, and columns with a subscript j

  • \(R_i\): category \(i\) of row variable \(R\)

  • \(C_j\): category \(j\) of column variable \(C\)

  • \(N\): total number of observations

  • \(f_{Oi}\): observed marginal frequency for row \(i\)

  • \(f_{Oj}\): observed marginal frequency for column \(j\)

  • \(f_{Eij}\): expected joint frequency for row \(i\) and column \(j\)

Expected Frequency

We next need to calculate expected frequencies based on \(H_0\)

To get expected frequencies, we use the observed marginal frequencies to determine the expected joint frequencies.

Thus, under the assumption of independence,

\[P(R_i \cap C_j) = P(R_i)P(C_j) = \bigg(\frac{f_{Oi}}{N}\bigg) \bigg(\frac{f_{Oj}}{N}\bigg) = \frac{f_{Oi}f_{Oj}}{N^2}\] \[f_{Eij} = N\times P(R_i \cap C_j) = N\bigg(\frac{f_{Oi}f_{Oj}}{N^2}\bigg) = \frac{f_{Oi}f_{Oj}}{N}\]

\(\chi^2\) Test of Independence

The \(\chi^2\) test statistic is computed in the same way as for the goodness of fit test: summing the normalized squared differences between observed and expected frequencies

\[\chi^2_{\text{obs}} = \sum_{i=1}^R \sum_{j=1}^C \frac{(f_{Oij} - f_{Eij})^2}{f_{Eij}}\]

  • sum over all cells

\[\frac{(33 - 22.72)^2}{22.72} + \frac{(33 - 43.28)^2}{43.28} + \frac{(251 - 261.28)^2}{261.28} + \frac{(508 - 497.72)^2}{497.72} = 7.71\]

Yates’ Correction

With small sample sizes, the \(\chi^2\) test of independence may be overly liberal

  • We will be more likely than intended to reject the null hypothesis even if it’s true

Yates’ correction attempts to correct for the “discontinuity” implied by having small frequencies

  • i.e., marginal probabilities that are closer to discrete (0 or 1 exactly) rather than continuous

\[\chi^2_{\text{obs}} = \sum_{i=1}^R \sum_{j=1}^C \frac{(|f_{Oij} - f_{Eij}| - 0.5)^2}{f_{Eij}}\]

Yates’ correction is conservative

  • We will be more likely than intended to retain the null hypothesis even if it’s false

The corrected \(p\)-value is necessarily higher than the uncorrected \(p\)-value

Father and Son

Ordinary \(\chi^2\) test developed by Karl Pearson

Egon Pearson is Karl’s son, another leader of early statistics!

Same \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) as Pearson’s \(\chi^2\) test of independence

Test statistic:

\[\chi^2(df) = \frac{\text{Pearson's } \chi^2 \times N}{N - 1}\]

  • \(\chi^2\): test statistic from ordinary test of independence

  • \(df\): \((R - 1) \times (C - 1)\)

  • \(N\) total frequency

Egon Pearson’s Adjusted \(\chi^2\) Test

E. Pearson’s adjusted \(\chi^2\) test statistic is always larger than Pearson’s \(\chi^2\)

  • \(p\)-value of E. Pearson’s test is always smaller than K. Pearson’s \(p\)-value

  • We say that E. Pearson’s test is liberal

  • E. Pearson’s test has higher statistical power to reject the false \(H_0\)

\[\chi^2_{\text{KP}} < \chi^2_{\text{EP}}\]

\[p_{_\text{KP}} > p_{_\text{EP}}\]

Slightly better performance when cell has a small expected frequency

Fisher’s Exact Test

Yates’ \(\chi^2\) and E. Pearson’s adjusted \(\chi^2\) change the calculation of \(\chi^2\). Fisher’s exact test uses another method to overcome limitations of the \(\chi^2\) test of independence.

  1. Treat the observed marginals as fixed

  2. Find all possible sets of cell counts

  3. Calculate the probability of each set of cell counts (hypergeometric distribution)

  4. Sum the probabilities corresponding to cell count sets that are more extreme (further from \(H_0\)) than the observed data \(\rightarrow\) this is the \(p\)-value

Limitations:

  • Complicated for tables larger than \(2 \times 2\)
  • Can be very slow when marginal frequencies are large

Recommendations by Campbell (2007)

  • For large sample sizes, the standard \(\chi^2\) test is best.

  • Default in R uses Yates’ correction, which helps with smaller samples and will have minimal cost in larger samples.

  • When at least one cell has expected frequency < 5, instead consider:

    • If one cell has expected frequency = 1, choose E. Pearson’s adjusted \(\chi^2\)

    • If all cells have expected frequency > 1, choose Fisher’s exact test

Campbell, I. (2007). Chi-square and Fisher-Irwin tests of two-by-two tables with small sample recommendations. Statistics in Medicine, 26, 3661-3675.
