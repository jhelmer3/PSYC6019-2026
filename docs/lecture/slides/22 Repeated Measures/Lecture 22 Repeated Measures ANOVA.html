Lecture 22: Repeated and Mixed ANOVA

Mark Himmelstein

Review

  • Independent Sample \(t\)-tests let us compare the means of two different groups
  • One way ANOVA lets us compare the means of more than two different groups
  • Factorial ANOVA lets us compare the means of more than two crossed factors

Put another way

  • Independent Sample \(t\)-tests let us test one between subjects factor with exactly two levels
  • One way ANOVA lets us test one between subjects factor with more than two levels
  • Factorial ANOVA lets us test more than one between subjects factor

Between vs. Within

Examples of between subjects factors

  • Randomizing participants into exactly one experimental condition
  • Mutually exclusive demographic categories at a given time point
  • Comparing students between different sections of the same course

Examples of within subjects factors

  • Having all participants go through all experimental conditions in a random order
  • Measurements from the same person collected at various time points
  • Comparing a group of students who each took the same courses

Assumption Violations

Which of our assumptions do within subjects designs violate?

  • Independence of observations

Where have we seen a method for handling this violation already?

  • Paired sample \(t\)-test
    • We simplified things by using a difference score so we only had one variable instead of two dependent variables
      • When we have more than two variables, this solution will no longer work

Stroop Effect

Traditional task:

BLUE      GREEN      RED      ORANGE

  • Difficult to correctly identify the text color of each word

Idea: maybe the Stroop effect has to do with contingency rather than congruency

  • i.e., Stroop task is hard because we have learned the correspondence between the word “blue” and the color “blue”
  • To test this hypothesis, manipulate contingencies between colors and neutral words

MEET      LOOP      CALL      MEET

MEET      MEET      SLID      CALL

FINS      MEET      LOOP      FINS

According to the contingency hypothesis, it should be difficult to correctly indicate “red” for MEET

Stroop Replication

Original study: Schmidt, J. R., & Besner, D. (2008). The Stroop effect: Why proportion congruent has nothing to do with congruency and everything to do with contingency. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning Memory, and Cognition, 34, 514–523.

OSF link: https://osf.io/x5b6d

Study design:

  • 432 trials, including 6 blocks of 72 trials, 6 words repeated 12 times per block
  • Each subject exposed to each type of trial
  • Subjects forced to respond quickly

IV: Contingency level: high, medium, low

DV: Proportion of trials where color was identified incorrectly

Key finding to replicate: Low contingency trials generate higher performance errors than medium contingency trials

The Structural Model

Simplest model:

\[y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_i + \tau_j + \epsilon_{ij}\]

  • \(\mu\): grand mean (overall average score)
  • \(\pi_{i}\): person’s \(i\)’s deviation from the average person
  • \(\tau_{j}\): treatment \(j\)’s deviation from the average treatment mean
  • \(\epsilon_{ij}\): error associated with person \(i\) and treatment \(j\)

With Interaction

\[y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_i + \tau_j + (\tau\pi)_{ij} + \epsilon_{ij}\]

  • \((\pi\tau)_{ij}\): allows different subjects to change differently over treatments
  • Can only be estimated if there are multiple measurements per person + condition combo
  • May not be the case in within-subjects designs

Notation

\[y_{ij} = \mu + \pi_i + \tau_j + (\tau\pi)_{ij} + \epsilon_{ij}\]

  • \(\mu\): overall average proportion of errors
  • \(\pi_i\): person \(i\)’s average deviation from the average person mean, across contingency levels
  • \(\tau_j\): sound condition \(j\)’s average deviation from the average sound condition mean
  • \((\tau\pi)_{ij}\): interaction of person with contingency level
    • If only one observation per cell, no way to distinguish interaction effect and error term
    • There is a \((\tau\pi)_{ij}\) corresponding to every observation, so \(\epsilon_{ij} = 0\)
    • Can describe every data point exactly

In such cases we instead work with the simpler model.

One-Way Repeated

Total sum of squares:

\[SST = \sum_i\sum_j(y_{ij} - \bar{y})^2\]

Sum of squares within subjects (treatment effect averaged across individuals):

\[SSA = I\sum_j (\bar{y}_{\bullet j} - \bar{y})^2\]

Sum of squares between subjects (variance in individual differences averaged across treatments):

\[SSS = J\sum_i (\bar{y}_{i\bullet} - \bar{y})^2\]

Sum of squares residual:

\[SSR = SST - SSS - SSA\]

  • \(I\): # subjects, \(i = 1, \ldots, I\)
  • \(J\): # treatment conditions, \(j = 1, \ldots, J\)

Refactor!

What if we wanted to test the effect of subject and condition? Then we would have a two-way ANOVA (without interaction) where we treat subjects as a second factor

Total sum of squares:

\[SST = \sum_i\sum_j(y_{ij} - \bar{y})^2\]

Sum of squares within subjects (treatment effect averaged across individuals):

\[SSA = I\sum_j (\bar{y}_{\bullet j} - \bar{y})^2\]

Sum of squares treating subjects as factor B:

\[SSB = J\sum_i (\bar{y}_{i\bullet} - \bar{y})^2\]

Sum of squares residual:

\[SSR = SST - SSA - SSB\]

  • \(I\): # subjects, \(i = 1, \ldots, I\)
  • \(J\): # treatment conditions, \(j = 1, \ldots, J\)

Fixed vs Random

Question: Do we care if the average error for one participant is different from the others?

  • We might, but probably not. We’re more interested in the effect of condition averaged over participants
  • But we also need to account for how the participant average effects contribute to our errors
  • Another way to think about this is that we need to estimate the variance in how people differ even if we aren’t testing that factor
  • This is sometimes referred to as a “random effect”
    • Fixed effects: A complete factor for which we want to compare all levels
    • Random effects: A factor where we sample from a population to estimate the variance attributable to that factor, but don’t care so much about the specific levels we sample from the population

Hypothesis Test

The hypothesis test is conducted using the same logic as the between-subjects ANOVA, except for the different error term:

\[F_{obs} = \frac{MSA}{MSR} = \frac{.042}{.002} = 23.42\]

Under \(H_0\) (all treatment means equal in the population):

\[F_{obs} \sim F(dfA, dfR)\]

Like other ANOVA tests, compare \(F_{obs}\) to the \(F\) distribution

  • numerator \(df\) = \(dfA\) = # within-subject treatment conditions - 1
  • denominator \(df\) = \(dfR\) = (# within-subject treatment conditions - 1)\(\times\)(# subjects - 1)

Assumptions: One Within Factor

  1. Independence

    • Individuals respond independently of each other
    • Residuals between subjects are independent of each other

  2. Covariance structure for the within-subjects factor

    • Compound symmetry (stronger)
      • Take the variance-covariance matrix of treatment effects
      • Equal variances: same number on the diagonal of the matrix
      • Equal covariances: same number on the off-diagonals
    • Sphericity (weaker)
      • Equal variances for all within-subjects variables

Violations of these assumptions can bias the \(F\)-statistic
