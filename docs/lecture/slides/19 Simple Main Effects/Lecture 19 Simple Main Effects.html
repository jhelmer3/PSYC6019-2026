Traditional Two-way ANOVA

For the two-way ANOVA, there are two categorical IVs (factors)

Factor \(A\) has levels \(j = 1, \ldots, J\) and Factor \(B\) has levels \(k = 1, \ldots, K\)

Population model:

\[y_{ijk} = \mu_G + \alpha_j + \beta_k + (\alpha\beta)_{jk} + \epsilon_{ijk}\]

  • \(y_{ijk}\): Observed value of observation \(i\) belonging to level \(j\) of factor \(A\) and level \(k\) of factor \(B\)
  • \(\mu_{G}\): Population grand mean (disregarding group membership)
  • \(\alpha_j = \mu_{A_j} - \mu_G\): effect of factor \(A_j\)
  • \(\beta_k = \mu_{B_k} - \mu_G\): effect of factor \(B_k\)
  • \((\alpha\beta)_{jk} = \mu_{AB_{jk}} - \mu_{A_j} - \mu_{B_k} + \mu_G\): interaction of factor \(A_j\) and factor \(B_k\)
  • \(\epsilon_{ijk}\): deviation of observation \(i\) from the mean of observations in both group \(A_j\) and group \(B_k\)

Omnibus Tests

Effect / Source Sums of Squares Degrees of Freedom Mean Squares \(F\)
Between-Gp Effect \(SS_{G}=\sum_{k}\sum_{j}[n_{jk}(\bar{y}_{jk}-\bar{y})^{2}]\) \(df_{G}=(J\times K-1)\) \(MS_{G}=\frac{SS_{G}}{df_{G}}\)
Main Effect \(A\) \(SS_{A}=nK\sum_{j}(\bar{y}_{j}-\bar{y})^{2}\) \(df_{A}=J-1\) \(MS_{A}=\frac{SS_{A}}{df_{A}}\) \(F(df_A, df_W) = \frac{MS_A}{MS_W}\)
Main Effect \(B\) \(SS_{B}=nJ\sum_{k}(\bar{y}_{k}-\bar{y})^{2}\) \(df_{B}=K-1\) \(MS_{B}=\frac{SS_{B}}{df_{B}}\) \(F(df_B, df_W) = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W}\)
Interaction Effect \(SS_{AB}=SS_{G}-SS_{A}-SS_{B}\) \(df_{AB}=(J-1)(K-1)\) \(MS_{AB}=\frac{SS_{AB}}{df_{AB}}\) \(F_{df_{AB}, df_W} = \frac{MS_{AB}}{MS_W}\)
Within-Gp Effect \(SS_{W}=SS_{T}-SS_{G}\) \(df_{W}=df_{T}-df_{G}\) \(MS_{W}=\frac{SS_{W}}{df_{W}}\)
Total Effect \(SS_{T}=\sum_{k}\sum_{j}\sum_{i}(y_{ijk}-\bar{y})^{2}\) \(df_{T}=N-1\) \(MS_{T}=\frac{SS_{T}}{df_{T}}\)

Interpretations

  • Interaction \(F_{df_{AB}, df_W}\): Do differences in means for factor \(A\) different depend on the level of \(B\) OR do differences in means for factor \(B\) different depend on the level of \(A\)
  • Main Effect \(A\) \(F(df_A, df_W)\): Are there differences in the group means for factor \(A\) at the average level of \(B\)
  • Main Effect \(B\) \(F(df_B, df_W)\): Are there differences in the group means for factor \(B\) at the average level of \(A\)

Like ommibus test in one-way ANOVA does not tell us how groups might differ, only whether there are differences in means and if those differences depend on one factor or both.

To investigate how groups differ, we need simple main effects

Simple Main Effects

Simple Main Effects are a way of looking at how the effects of one factor differ for different levels of the other.

  • We can investigate the simple main effect of factor \(A\) on the outcome, separately in each group of factor \(B\)
  • Equivalently, we can investigate the simple main effect of factor \(B\) on the outcome, separately in each group of factor \(A\)
  • Either procedure is sufficient, no need to do both
  • Choosing which one to look at depends primarily on if there is a particular interpretation you prefer

Simple Main Effects: Conceptual Method

Suppose we want to run simple effects for main effect \(A\)

Step 1: Run a separate one-way ANOVA for each level of factor \(B\)

Step 2: Record the sums of squares, degrees of freedom, and mean squares of the main effect for each one-way ANOVA

Step 3: Use the mean squares within and \(df\) within from the original factorial ANOVA

Step 4: Hypothesis Testing (next slide)

Simple Main Effects: Hypothesis Testing

\(H_0\): there is no simple main effect in the population

\(H_1\): there is a simple main effect in the population

Test statistic:

\[F(df_{\text{simple}}, df_W) = \frac{MS_{\text{simple}}}{MS_W}\]

When \(H_0\) is true, test statistic follows the \(F\) distribution with \(df_{\text{simple}}\) numerator and \(df_W\) degrees of freedom

We can conduct multiple comparisons between pairs of groups of factor \(A\), separately in groups of factor \(B\)

  • We can use the procedures that we learned about last week to control the Type I error rate (e.g., Tukey HSD)

Multiple comparisons are not necessary when there are only two levels of a factor

Simple Main Effects

If we assume that the interaction isn’t meaningful then we may want to look at the simple main effects

  • What is meaningful?
  • Often interpreted as “significant”
    • Personally don’t love this way of thinking! Significance doesn’t always mean an effect is “real”
  • More importantly: If differences in one factor change for different levels of another factor, it may not make sense to interpret simple main effects
    • Because simple main effects average over all levels of other factors

Example

For example, let’s look at pairwise comparisons for the main effect of Method averaged over the effects of both sides of the hill

em2 <- emmeans(a1, specs = "method")
pairs(em2)
 contrast    estimate    SE  df t.ratio p.value
 control - a    -5.93 0.525 294 -11.304  <.0001
 control - b    -6.14 0.525 294 -11.705  <.0001
 a - b          -0.21 0.525 294  -0.400  0.9155

Results are averaged over the levels of: hill 
P value adjustment: tukey method for comparing a family of 3 estimates

At the average of east and west hills - control is significantly different from a - control is significantly different from b - a and b are not significantly different from each other

Semantic Clarification

  • Simple Main effects: The main effect of one factor at different levels of the other factor
    • e.g. the effect of Factor A at each level of Factor B separately
    • This is how/when you probe the interaction effect
  • Simple main effects (sometimes called marginal effects or ordinary main effects): The main effect of one factor averaged over all of the levels of the other factor.
    • e.g. the effect of Factor A averaged over all levels of Factor B
    • This is how/when you interpret/probe the main effects directly

Suggested Procedures

  • First, investigate the interaction effect
  • If significant, or effect size of interaction is large, investigate the simple main effect of factor \(A\) on outcome, separately in each group in factor \(B\)
    • Equivalently, you could investigate the simple main effect of factor \(B\) on outcome, separately in each group in factor \(A\)
    • Either procedure is sufficient
  • Consider whether it is meaningful to average over all levels of a factor before considering any interpretations of main effects
    • Does your data adequately represent all possible factors you want to generalize about?
    • Is the interaction effect non-significant/small?
    • If yes to either, main effects can be sensible

Effect Size

Standardized associations:

  • \(\eta^2\) and partial \(\eta^2\)
  • \(\omega^2\) and partial \(\omega^2\)

Similar to calculations and interpretations for interaction and main effects

\(\eta^2\) for simple main effect

\[\frac{SS_{\text{simple}}}{SS_{T}}\]

\(\omega^2\) for simple main effect

\[\frac{df_{\text{simple}}(MS_{\text{simple }}-MS_{W})}{MS_{W}+SS_{T}}\]

Partial \(\eta^2\) for simple main effect

\[\frac{SS_{\text{simple}}}{SS_{\text{simple}}+SS_{W}}\]

Partial \(\omega^2\) for simple main effect

\[\frac{df_{\text{simple}}(MS_{\text{simple}}-MS_{W})}{SS_{\text{simple}}+(N-df_{\text{simple}})MS_{W}}\]

Post-Hoc Comparisons

Cohen’s \(d\) between groups \(k\) and \(l\):

\[d = \frac{\bar{y}_k - \bar{y}_l}{s}\]

  • \(\bar{y}_k\): sample mean of group \(k\)
  • \(\bar{y}_l\): sample mean of group \(l\)

Options for \(s\):

  • Sample \(SD\) of group \(k\) (focal interest in group \(k\))
  • Sample \(SD\) of group \(l\) (focal interest in group \(l\))
  • \(\sqrt{MS_W}\): \(SD\) of within-group effect

Example

We can calculate \(d\) for the difference between a and control on the east side of the hill. The emmeans object we saved before can give us the cell means

 east control   85.4 0.525 294     84.4     86.5
 west control   81.8 0.525 294     80.8     82.9
 east a         91.1 0.525 294     90.1     92.2
 west a         88.0 0.525 294     86.9     89.0
 east b         89.5 0.525 294     88.5     90.6

I’ll use \(\sqrt{MS_W} = \sqrt{13.8}\) as my denominator

\[d_{\text{a} - \text{control}} = \frac{91.1 - 85.4}{\sqrt{13.8}} = 1.53\]

Interaction Contrasts

Allow us to test whether whether a linear contrast for one factor differs as a function of a linear contrast for another

Example: Is the difference between method A and method B on hill A the same as the difference between Method A and method B on hill B

Setting Up Contrasts

First we need to set up the relevant contrasts for both factors as separate lists (you can have several different contrasts for each factor)

Then we use contrast() and specify the interaction as a list with both of our lists
