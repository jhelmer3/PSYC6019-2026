Polynomial Regression

Polynomial equations:

\[\begin{aligned} \hat{y} &= b_{0}+b_{1}x &&\quad \text{1st order polynomial (linear)}\\ \hat{y} &= b_{0}+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2} &&\quad \text{2nd order polynomial (quadratic)}\\ \hat{y} &= b_{0}+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}+b_{3}x^{3} &&\quad \text{3rd order polynomial (cubic)}\\ \hat{y} &= b_{0}+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2}+\cdots+b_{p}x^{p} &&\quad p\text{th order polynomial} \end{aligned}\]

  • Always include all “lower order” terms
  • A polynomial of order \(p\) has \((p-1)\) bends or inflections

\(p = 1\) has no bends (straight line)

Quadratic polynomial:

  • One bend
  • \(b_2\): quadratic effect
  • \(b_2 > 0\): curve is concave up
  • \(b_2 < 0\): curve is concave down

Centering

Two options for setting up \(x\) in the equation:

\[\begin{aligned} \hat{y} &= b_{0}+b_{1}x+b_{2}x^{2} &&\quad \text{uncentered equation}\\ \hat{y} &= b_{0}+b_{1}(x-\bar{x})+b_{2}(x-\bar{x})^{2} &&\quad \text{centered equation} \end{aligned}\]

Regression coefficients between uncentered and centered equations:

  • \(b_2\): second-order coefficient (quadratic effect)
    • Both equations have exactly the same \(b_2\) coefficient, standard error of \(b_2\), and \(t\)-test
  • \(b_1\): first-order coefficient
    • \(b_1\) differs between the uncentered and centered equations
    • \(b_1\) depends on the scaling of \(b_2\)
  • \(b_0\): regression constant (intercept)
    • \(b_0\) differs between the uncentered and centered equations
    • \(b_0\) depends on the scaling of \(b_2\)

Note: no need to center the outcome \(y\)

Centering and Colinearity

Using centered \(x\) eliminates a potential computational difficulty (depending on the accuracy of your software/computer)

  • collinearity between \(x\) and \(x^2\)

Example:

\(x\) \(x^{2}\) \((x-\bar{x})\) \((x-\bar{x})^{2}\)
1 1 -3 9
3 9 -1 1
5 25 1 1
7 49 3 9

\[\begin{aligned} \text{cor}(x, x^2) &= .98\\ \text{cor}[(x - \bar{x}), (x - \bar{x})^2] &= 0 \end{aligned}\]

  • Merely changing the scale of \(x\) dramatically changes \(\text{cor}(x, x^2)\)

  • The correlation that is produced merely by scaling is called non-essential multicollinearity

  • If \(x\) is symmetric, \((x - \bar{x})\) and \((x - \bar{x})^2\) will have zero correlation

  • If \(x\) is skewed, \((x - \bar{x})\) and \((x - \bar{x})^2\) will have non-zero correlation

Quadratic Example

The ^ operator has a special function that is not “raise to the power of” when used inside R’s formula syntax. To “inhibit” this special meaning, we need to wrap the quadratic term around I()

mod2 <- lm(collegeGrad ~ black + I(black^2), MplsDemo)
summary(mod2)
